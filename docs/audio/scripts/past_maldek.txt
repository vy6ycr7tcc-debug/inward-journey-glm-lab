They say there was once a world where we now see only rubble. Between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter, telescopes find a scattered river of stone turning slowly in the dark, too faint for any eye to see, but there all the same. Astronomers count more than a million rocky bodies out there, and all of them together weigh less than a small fraction of our moon.

The old telling goes that this was not always debris. It was a planet. It had a name. And it broke.

Whether history or mirror, the story is worth hearing. The tradition tells it the way you would speak of a relative nobody mentions at dinner. Not with relish. With gravity. Because the people of that world were not monsters. They were choosers, like us.

The telling says they made a choice, and kept making it, until the choice was complete. Not in a single dramatic hour, but the way a river carves a canyon, day by day, decision by decision, each one small enough to defend. A little more for me, a little less for my neighbor, and then a little more again. They chose themselves over each other, and then over everything, until there was nothing left to choose except the ending. And the ending, when it came, was total. The planet destroyed itself. The tradition is spare about the mechanism, and lavish about the meaning.

Picture them at their height, if the telling is true. Cities lit like constellations. Machines that could have healed every wound. And the slow drift underneath it all. The strong taking a little more. The rest learning not to look. No villain ever stood up and announced the end of the world. It was only ever the small decision, made on an ordinary day, and then made again. That is what makes the story frightening. Not that they were different from us. That they were not.

And here is the moral, stated plainly, because the old teachers always state it plainly in the end. Hatred has an address. It does not stay in the heart, where you think you are keeping it. It moves outward, into the hands, into the tools, into the world, and if it is fed long enough, it will consume the very ground you stand on. What hatred does to a home, first the home of the heart, then the home of the world, is the whole of the lesson.

Look at the memorial again, that slow river of stone. Consider what it means that the universe keeps it. Nothing is wasted, not even wreckage. The broken world was not deleted. It was kept, in pieces. Every astronomer who charts those stones is reading a textbook the universe refused to throw away. And it is not only astronomers who read it. Every civilization that comes to the fork, where power outruns wisdom, finds the same stones waiting. Patient as grief. Saying without words, we have seen this choice before.

The telling ends the way these tellings always end. Not with a verdict on the dead, but with a question for the living. The stones are still turning up there. They are patient. They can wait while we decide what kind of world we intend to be. The choice that broke a planet is the same choice offered to each of us, scaled down to the size of a single day. Whom will you serve. What will you feed. The memorial is not there to frighten you. It is there to remind you that the choosing matters. And that even now, it is not too late to choose otherwise.
