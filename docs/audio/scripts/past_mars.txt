The old telling goes like this. Before there was us, there was them. On the red planet next door, the one you can see on a clear night burning like an ember, there was once air enough to breathe and water enough to gather. And there were people.

History, the patient kind, has since found its own version of the water. Our rovers have photographed stones worn round by running water, and the dry beds of lakes that vanished ages ago. The Romans named the planet for their god of war centuries before any machine touched it. And the people, over the long ages, developed a particular talent, the way some families develop a talent for music. Their talent was war.

It is said they grew so skilled at it, so devoted to it, that they did what no enemy could have done for them. They ruined their own sky. The atmosphere, thinned and poisoned by endless conflict, could no longer hold the conditions of life. A whole world, made unlivable not by asteroid or ice age, but by its own inhabitants, practicing their talent on each other until there was nothing left to practice on.

Whether history or mirror, stay with the image, because the tradition that carries this story refuses to let it be merely ancient. It says the people of the red world did not simply vanish. The telling says they were lifted out of their ruined world in the middle of their lessons, carried here by the compassion of older and wiser minds, and set down on this Earth to continue the coursework. To try again. To learn, at last, the ways of love.

Read that again slowly, because it changes the color of everything. The tradition says that a great portion of humanity carries this old warlikeness in its blood and bone. Not as a curse. As unfinished homework. The tendency you see in the news, the ancient reflex to answer hurt with hurt, the way nations still reach for the fist, this is not proof that we are broken. It is proof that we are students, mid lesson, working on the exact curriculum we failed before.

And the curriculum has a name, though the old teachers rarely say it outright. They describe it instead as a choice between two hands. The open hand says, we are one, so your suffering is mine to ease. The closed fist says, I am the one who matters, and the world will bend. Every war ever fought was the fist, scaled up. Every act of mercy was the hand, scaled down to the size of a single gesture. The red world, in the telling, chose the fist until the fist had nothing left to strike but the air itself.

Now the moral of the story, and the tradition is unsparing here. The cost of the closed fist is always paid, and it is always paid by the one who makes it. Not as punishment. As physics. A fist cannot hold anything. It can only strike, and every strike lands, eventually, on the striker's own world. Mars did not lose a war. It won every war, right up until there was no one left to fight and no air left to breathe. That is what victory looks like when the fist is the only tool.

But the story does not end in the desert. That is the point of the telling. The desert is the middle. The tradition insists that no one is discarded, not even a whole planet of warriors. They were carried here, mid lesson, and given the one thing the red dunes could no longer offer. Another chance. The classroom moved. The students remained. And the lesson on the board is the same one it has always been.

So when you feel the old reflex rise in you, the heat behind the eyes, the certainty that force will settle it, remember the red world. Remember that the fist has been tried, at planetary scale, by people as clever and as brave as we are, and that we know exactly how it ends. It ends in a desert, beautiful and silent, waiting for someone to learn what it has to teach.

The choice is still open. It is open right now, in whatever small war you are fighting today, with whomever you are fighting it. Open the hand. The universe has run this experiment before. It is telling you the results in advance, out of something very much like love.
